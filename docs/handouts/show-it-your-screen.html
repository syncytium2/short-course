Show It Your Screen

You do not need to become an expert at web deployment. Nobody stays one — the dashboards are rebuilt faster than anybody can memorise them. What you need is a loop, and the loop is four beats long.

This one was walked, and it is the only page here that was. Every stoppage below happened on 2026-08-30, in one sitting, to two people putting a first site on the internet. The screens are real screens. Nothing is illustrative.

The finding this page exists for. Across that walk the guide wrote instructions in advance five times and was wrong five times — every click path, every field name, even the settings it had called “the stable part.” The same guide then unstuck every one of those within a single exchange, the moment it could see the screen.

That gap is the whole lesson. An agent is a poor map and an excellent guide. Ask it what the buttons will say and it will confidently invent them. Show it what the buttons do say and it will read them better than you can. Stop asking for the map. Start sending the view.

1 · Why nobody is an expert at this

the premise

It is tempting to think there is a person who knows the deployment dashboard, and that your problem is not being them. There is not. The dashboard is rebuilt on the vendor's schedule, not yours, and it is rebuilt for reasons that have nothing to do with you: products get merged, interfaces get redesigned, and — the one that catches everybody — features roll out to some accounts before others.

That last one matters more than staleness. When your screen disagrees with a written guide, the ordinary assumption is that the guide is out of date. Sometimes it is. But sometimes both are current and describing different accounts, and no amount of re-reading will ever resolve that. Only looking at your own screen does.

So the skill is not knowing where the button is. The skill is being unbothered when it moves.

None of this is about being non-technical. The guide in this walk had read the vendor's current documentation minutes earlier and still got the path wrong four times running, because the documentation described a dashboard neither of them was looking at.

2 · The loop

four beats
  1. 1

    Try the thing.

    Do not read ahead, and do not ask for the full path first — you will be given one, and it will be wrong. Click the most plausible button and see what happens. You cannot break a deployment dashboard by pressing the wrong thing; the worst case is a project you delete afterwards.

  2. 2

    Notice you are stuck — and notice it early.

    Stuck is: the screen does not match what you were told, a field you were not expecting is asking for something, or an error appeared. Ten seconds is long enough to be stuck. The cost of asking is one message; the cost of guessing is a wrong setting you will not connect to the failure twenty minutes later.

  3. 3

    Screenshot the whole screen, and send it.

    Not a crop of the bit you think matters — the whole thing. In this walk the deciding detail was twice in a part of the screen the person would not have cropped in: once a green dot on an icon, once a greyed link at the very bottom of a card. See §3.

  4. 4

    Do exactly the one next thing, then look again.

    One step, not the plan. The next screen will not be what either of you expected, and a five-step instruction is four wrong steps stacked behind one right one. Done when you can say what changed on screen — not what you were told would change.

Then repeat, and expect to. This walk went round that loop seven times to publish one page. That is not a sign anything went wrong. It is what the job is.

3 · What to send, and what it is worth

the craft

A screenshot on its own gets you an answer. A screenshot plus three sentences gets you the right one.

SendBecause
The whole window You do not know which part matters — that is why you are stuck. Twice in this walk the answer was in furniture the person had no reason to think was relevant.
What you clicked to get here The same screen can be reached two ways and mean different things. One word saves an exchange.
What you expected The gap between expected and actual is the diagnosis. Often you have not hit an error at all — you are on a different, working path, and only the expectation is wrong.
Error text, as text Copy it verbatim rather than paraphrasing. The exact wording is what can be searched and matched; a summary of an error is a different error.
“This doesn't match what you said” Say it plainly. It is the single most useful sentence in this whole walk. It ends the guessing immediately and costs you nothing — being wrong about a dashboard is not embarrassing for either of you.

What not to bother with. Cropping. Annotating with arrows. Apologising. Retyping what the screen says. Working out the cause before you ask — that is the part you are delegating.

One thing to check before you send. A screenshot of a dashboard can carry an account id, an email address, a billing figure, or a token. Glance at the edges. Nothing in this walk needed to be hidden, but the glance is cheap and the alternative is not.

4 · Seven stoppages, in order, from one afternoon

the evidence

Publishing was budgeted at twenty minutes and described as the easy part. This is what actually happened. Three were solved by the loop. Four were not, and saying which is which is the honest half of the claim.

01 The dashboard was down, and the status page said it wasn't not the loop
The person

“again! The Cloudflare Dashboard is temporarily unavailable.”

The guide checked the vendor's status page: one active incident, an unrelated product. Dashboard not listed. The document said fine; the system said no.

The right move is not to re-read the status page. It is to believe the screen. A status page is a document maintained by people who are currently busy with the outage.

Unstuck by: waiting, and trying again. No advice helped. Cost: the whole first attempt.
02 The guide was refused permission mid-task not the loop

Asked to make a repository public, the agent's own safety layer refused — and then refused the harmless read-only command after it. The person had already said yes.

This is worth expecting rather than being surprised by. An agent is not a single thing that either can or cannot act; it sits inside limits it does not control and cannot always predict. When it stops, the useful response is the one it should give you: what it was trying to do, and the command you can run yourself.

Unstuck by: handing the action back to the person. Note: this stoppage was never resolved — the route went around it.
03 Every click path was wrong, four times running the loop
The person

“i need the cloudflare steps. it doesn't match what you posted”

The guide had written Workers & Pages → Create application → Pages → Connect to Git, from the vendor's current documentation. That path no longer existed. Checking again turned up something worse: the vendor's own docs disagreed with each other, because Pages was being folded into Workers and different pages had been updated at different times.

Unstuck by: one screenshot. One exchange, after four failed attempts to describe the path in advance.
04 The settings did not appear at all the loop

The guide had insisted that although click paths move, the settings are stable, and handed over a table of them. The next screen asked for a project name and nothing else.

Unstuck by: one screenshot. Guide's record at this point: five predictions, five wrong.
05 The same screen, a moment later, had four more fields the loop
The person

“revised screen”

The form had finished loading. Now it showed a build command, a deploy command already filled in with npx wrangler deploy, a checkbox, and a toggle.

Unstuck by: the person re-sending unprompted. This is beat 3 used well — not waiting to be asked.
06 The pre-filled command needed a file nobody mentioned the loop, ahead of the failure

The most valuable moment of the walk, and it is not a rescue. Reading screenshot 3, the guide recognised that npx wrangler deploy requires a wrangler.jsonc in the repository — and the repository had two files, neither of them that. Nothing on the screen said so. The command arrives pre-filled, looking exactly like a default that works.

The guide

“Stop before Deploy — that npx wrangler deploy will fail.”

The config was written, pushed, and checked with a dry run before anything was clicked. Nine lines:

{ "name": "route-test", "compatibility_date": "2026-08-30", "assets": { "directory": "./public/" } }

This is what the guide is actually for. Not knowing where buttons are — it had just failed at that five times — but recognising, from a screenshot, a failure that had not happened yet and would have given no usable message when it did.

Unstuck by: the screenshot, before the error existed. A beginner would have clicked Deploy and been told nothing useful.
07 It said it worked, which is not the same as working the habit

The build went green: four stages, 41 seconds, every tick present. That is a report, and an action and its report are two different events.

So the page was fetched: 200, 6242 bytes, text/html — and then the body was checked for the page's own title, its buttons, and the name of its first record. A 404 can return 200 and a placeholder is still bytes. Only the third check distinguishes a live site from a live server.

Not a stoppage. Included because it is the one step nobody is tempted to do and the only one that proves anything.

5 · What your guide will get wrong, so you can price it

calibration

You are being asked to lean on something that was wrong five times in one afternoon. That is a reasonable thing to be uneasy about, and the resolution is not trust — it is knowing which half to trust.

Ask it forExpect
A click path, in advance Wrong, confidently. It is describing a remembered or documented interface, not yours. It will not hedge, because it does not know it is out of date.
Field values, in advance Better, and still unreliable — in this walk the fields it named did not exist on one screen and were differently named on the next.
What is on this screenshot Reliable. Every one of the seven was read correctly first time.
What this error means Reliable, and the thing it is genuinely better at than you — including errors that have not happened yet, which is stoppage 06.
Whether it worked Ask it to check, not to conclude. Fetch the page, read the body. It will tell you a thing succeeded on the same evidence you have, which is no evidence.

Say when it is wrong. It does not mind and the correction is fast. The most productive sentence in this entire walk was six words: “it doesn't match what you posted.” Everything after that went quickly.

6 · Where the loop does not help

honest scope

Four of the seven were not solved by showing anyone a screen, and a page that claimed otherwise would be selling something.

KindWhat actually resolves it
The vendor is down Waiting. Believe your screen over the status page, and go and do something else.
A permission boundary You do it. The guide should hand you the exact command and stop, rather than find a way around — a guide that routes around its own limits is the wrong kind of helpful.
A decision only you can make Public or private, what it is called, what it costs. Not the guide's to choose, and it should say so.
Anything that spends money Read it yourself. This walk stayed on free tiers throughout, which was a decision, not an accident.

And the count is the point. Three of seven by the loop is not a disappointing number. Those three had already resisted five careful attempts to write them down in advance, and they each fell in one exchange. The other four were never the kind of thing an instruction sheet could have covered either.

7 · What this leaves you able to say

the claim

Not “I can deploy a website.” The dashboard will have moved by the time you do it again, and that sentence will be false in a way you cannot feel.

What you can say is: I can get a website deployed, from a screen I have never seen, without knowing in advance where anything is. That claim survives the redesign. It survives changing vendor. It is the one worth having, and it is the only one this afternoon actually established.

The site that came out of it was one HTML file, a config of nine lines, and seven stoppages. It is on the internet.
