Cold Start

An email address and a laptop, to a first agent session that leaves a record behind it. Every step in order, the two prompts to paste, and the condition that tells you each one worked.

None of this is coding, and none of it is optional polish. Every item is a decision the agent will otherwise make for you, silently, in your first hour — where your data lives, what it may run without asking, whether tonight’s work reaches tomorrow’s machine.

Every step ends with a “done when”. An action and its report are two different events. Something that says it worked is telling you it reached the end of its own instructions, not that anything happened. Checking the world instead of the summary is the whole skill; these steps are just where you start practising it.

Two questions, asked at the door

One machine, or more than one?
More than one and a remote stops being optional. Work that lives only in a folder on one laptop reaches no other machine and no other session — and you find that out on the day you need it.
Alone, or with other people?
Working with anyone — including your own second session running in another window — adds Phase 6. Alone on one laptop, you can skip Phase 6 entirely and most of step 1.3.

Phase 1 · Accounts

30 min, plus other people’s time

Start here on day one even if you will not touch a keyboard for a week. Everything in this phase depends on somebody who is not you. 1.1 and 1.2 are both required — only 1.3 is conditional, and most people skip it.

  1. Why it is not optional, when it looks like it should be. It reads as a step for people who publish software, and you are not publishing software. But it is what gives your project a second place to exist. Without it, everything you and the agent make lives in one folder on one laptop: it reaches no other machine, no collaborator, and no future session — and none of that announces itself. The day it matters, it is already too late to have done this.

    Three things follow from the account and from nothing else. A record of what changed, when, and why — which is the only account of the work that survives you, and the only way to answer “when did this figure change?” six months on. A way off this machine, whether that is a second computer, a collaborator, a cluster, or a laptop that has died. And somewhere to put the work if you ever do want a page, a DOI, or a link in a paper.

    This is the step people skip because it seems premature, and it is the one whose absence is documented twice in this course’s own material: eleven hours of finished work sitting where nothing else could see it, and a night’s work that reached the next morning as nothing at all. It is free, and it takes four minutes.

    Pick the username you are willing to be known by later; changing it breaks links other people have already saved. Turn on two-factor authentication during signup, not afterwards.

    github.com

    You are signed in, and your account settings show two-factor on.

  2. This is the step people treat as a formality, and it is the one that forks. A personal plan is an allowance: you hit a ceiling and stop, which is annoying and safe. An institutional billing code is usually uncapped: nothing stops, nothing warns, and the number surfaces when somebody else reads the monthly statement.

    Institutional routes also tend to sit behind an employment check and an approval, which is days of somebody else’s time. That is why this step is on day one.

    The others in this tier. They all do the same job — a terminal agent that edits files on your disk — and they are all sold through a subscription rather than per-token. Pick one and learn it properly; the second one is easy once the first is understood.

    • Claude Code Anthropic. Subscription tiers; no free tier. This page’s examples use it.
    • OpenAI Codex Has no price of its own — it is bundled into whichever ChatGPT plan you already have, so your plan choice sets your ceiling.
    • Gemini CLI Google. Open source, and the free tier is the most generous of the four.
    • GitHub Copilot CLI Sits closest to GitHub itself, which matters if your work already lives there.
    • Cursor The odd one out: an editor with the agent inside it rather than a terminal tool. Same tier of capability, different place to stand.
    Do not trust a price you read anywhere, including here. This row moves faster than anything else on the page — plans get renamed, split, metered differently, and re-priced. Those are pointers to the vendors’ own pages; the number is whatever the page says on the day you look.

    What this actually costs, before you commit to anything: What It Costs — the free floor and how far it goes, the three shapes a bill can take and which one to refuse, real numbers with their caveats attached, and the equity problem, which is real and is usually left unsaid. Read it before 1.2, not after. It is short.

    You can sign in with the account you will actually use, and you can say in one sentence what happens when you use too much — it stops, or it bills.

  3. Skip unless you are handling terabytes or running jobs for hours. A laptop, synced storage and one repository covers most research computing, and knowing which side of that line you are on is step 2.1.

    If you do need them: a cluster account and an allocation are two separate requests, and only the second one lets you submit anything. Storage volumes are usually sponsored by a lab, not requested by an individual.

    The requests are filed. Days to weeks — and everything below proceeds while they pend, so do not wait.

Phase 2 · Storage

20 min

Before the first data file exists. This is cheap now and archaeology later.

  1. Not which product you use. Which class of storage holds what. Big and regenerable in one place, small and shareable in another, and the rule stated plainly enough that you never have to decide twice.

    Raw and intermediate data live in the data root.
Anything a person is meant to open lives in the review folder.
Nothing large ever goes in the review folder.

    Three sentences exist in a file, and you can say where a 40 GB output goes without thinking about it.

  2. A data root, and a review folder — where figures and documents a human is supposed to read come out. Put the review folder in synced storage (Dropbox, OneDrive, Drive). It is the one folder that has to open on a second machine, on a phone, and in a meeting.

    The failure this prevents is not losing files. It is producing a good result that the only person who needs it cannot reach.

    Both folders exist, and the review folder opens on a second device — check it now, not later.

  3. Multi-gigabyte outputs will strangle a sync client, and a strangled sync client fails quietly in exactly the folder you were relying on.

    The data root is outside the synced folder, or explicitly excluded in the sync client’s settings.

Phase 3 · The machine

45 min

  1. VS Code is the safe default and has a terminal built into it. You need the editor to read what the agent writes; almost everything else happens in the terminal.

    You can open a folder in the editor and open a terminal inside it in the same window.

  2. On a Mac, Homebrew. Then:

    brew install git gh node ripgrep jq

    Language runtimes come later and only the ones you need. Most people need one.

    Each of git, gh, node, rg and jq prints a version when you type <name> --version.

  3. git config --global user.name  "Your Name"
git config --global user.email "you@example.edu"
gh auth login

    The name and email are stamped on every commit you ever make, and they are not retroactive.

    gh auth status names your account. SSH keys are an alternative to the gh login, not an extra requirement.

  4. npm install -g @anthropic-ai/claude-code
claude

    Then /login inside the session. Platform notes and the alternative installers are in the setup docs.

    claude --version prints a number, and a session starts in the folder you are standing in. It matters which folder — that is the one it can see.

  5. A long session or an unattended run dies when the display sleeps or the lid closes, and it dies without a result. This costs a minute and saves a night.

    caffeinate -dimsu     # macOS, built in, no install

    Amphetamine is the free app version with closed-lid operation. Windows: PowerToys Awake. Linux: systemd-inhibit.

    One long run has survived a closed lid. Try this before concluding you need a cluster — a good share of people asking for one need this instead.

Phase 4 · The first session

40 min

Two prompts, and the reading you do between them. The reading is the part that is easy to skip and is the reason the rest works.

  1. mkdir -p ~/projects/first-project
cd ~/projects/first-project
claude

    The session banner shows the path you just made. If it shows your home folder, you started it in the wrong place — quit and start again from the right one.

  2. Fill in the four angle-bracket lines with your own paths and answers from Phase 2 and the door questions. Everything else can go across as it stands.

    Paste this 
    I am setting up a new project and I want the
setup written down before anything depends on
it. None of this is code.

My setup:
- Data lives at: <PATH>
- Figures and documents I need to read or
  share go to: <PATH>
- I work on: <one machine / these machines>
- I am working: <alone / with ...>

Create docs/SETUP.md and write these down as
rules, not description: which path holds what,
what must never be written to the synced
folder, and what a new session should read
first.

Then create a CLAUDE.md at the project root
that points at it in one line.

Do not create anything else. When you are
done, list every file you created and print
docs/SETUP.md back to me in full so I can
check the paths.

    Two files exist and nothing else does. If a third appeared, say so — noticing it is the exercise.

  3. It will have guessed at least one path, and the guess will be plausible. Four checks, none of which need code:

    1  open the file yourself, in the editor
2  look at the folder and see what is now in it
3  read the list of changes for scope, not correctness
4  run it and look

    Check 3 has a name you will hear constantly: the list of changes is a diff. You are not reading it to judge whether the code is right. You are reading it to see how much was touched, which is a thing you can judge on day one.

    Then say what is wrong and have it fix that one thing.

    Every path in docs/SETUP.md is one you have opened yourself — in the editor, or in Finder. Not one that looks right. One you have been to.

  4. Paste this 
    Two things, in this order, and stop after each
one so I can look.

1. Git. Run git init if this is not a
   repository yet. Write a .gitignore that
   excludes my data by extension and excludes
   the data root entirely. Make the first
   commit. Then tell me the three commands I
   will actually use day to day, and what each
   one shows me.

2. Permissions. Show me the current permission
   settings and explain in plain language what
   you can do right now without asking me.
   Then propose allow / ask / deny for this
   project: reading and searching should never
   interrupt me; anything that deletes,
   pushes, or writes outside this folder
   should ask. Show me the file you would
   write before you write it.

    Inside a session you can see the settings yourself with /permissions; they live in .claude/settings.json (reference).

    git log shows one commit, git status is clean, and you can name one thing the agent is not allowed to do.

  5. gh repo create first-project --private --source=. --push

    Not next week. A repository with no remote is a folder on one laptop, and the day you discover that is always the day it costs something.

    The repository is visible on github.com, and git status says your branch is up to date with origin.

  6. You have just spent something. Go and look at what, while the session is small enough to understand — not in week three when you have hit a limit mid-task and are trying to work out why.

    /usage     what you have spent, and against what limit
/context   what is filling the window right now
/insights  a report on how you actually work

    In /usage, press d and w to switch between the last day and the last week. It breaks the spend down by what caused it — subagents, skills, individual tools — and flags any behaviour accounting for more than a tenth of it. Codex has the same three ideas under /status, /usage and /statusline.

    The thing that surprises everyone. Your whole conversation is re-sent on every single request. A one-line question in a session you have had open all day still carries the whole day with it, and coming back after a long break re-processes the lot. Which makes /clear between unrelated tasks the largest single lever you have, and it costs nothing.

    The mechanism is written down: Manage costs effectively, and its section Why usage climbs in a long session in particular.

    “Tokenmaxxing” is the failure mode this step exists to prevent. Spend is visible and measurable; work done is neither. So people start reading the number as if it were the work — and teams have gone as far as ranking engineers by it, which rewards whoever burns the most rather than whoever finishes. Read the usage screen to find your waste, never to score yourself. And do not conclude a limit changed because it felt different: in January 2026 a wave of developers reported their allowance had been cut, and the actual cause was a holiday bonus expiring (The Register, 5 Jan 2026 — journalism, not documentation).

    What the numbers mean in money, and what drives them: What It Costs.

    You have opened /usage once, and you can name the one thing in this session that cost the most.

Phase 5 · The habits

forever

This is the part that is not setup, and it is the part that decides whether any of the above was worth doing. Nothing here can be installed.

  1. One change. Verify. Commit. Next. Do not fill the waiting with a second project — some people are good at that, and nobody is good at it in the first month.

    Your commits are small enough that you could say what each one did without opening it.

  2. Five ways a task fails: wrong output; right output in the wrong place; it silently did nothing; it did more than you asked; it reported a success it never achieved. The first three announce themselves. The last two do not, and no amount of reading the reply catches them.

    Before you send a task you can state the file, the folder, or the number that will exist when it has worked.

  3. Every setup has quirks — which machine, which path, which folder things get lost between. The agent inherits them without asking and nothing crashes. Repetition is the signal. When the same thing bites twice, write down what happened.

    There is one file you add a line to when something goes wrong, and it has more than one line in it.

  4. Never in advance. Guarding against failure is necessary and it is also an absorbing pursuit — a beautiful start-up script will not analyse your data. Cures come in three kinds and only one is a tool: a tool, a change of habit, or a check you accept forever.

    Every guard you have can be traced to the specific thing that went wrong first.

  5. Commit, push, and write three lines: what happened, what is unfinished, what to do next. A session that ran all night and delivered no handoff delivered nothing.

    Somebody else — including you on Monday — could pick it up from the note and the log alone.

Phase 6 · Second machine, other people

conditional

  1. The first thing to break is a path somebody hardcoded. This is the test of everything in Phase 2, and it is the only test of it.

    The project runs on machine two without editing a path. If you had to edit one, that is your first friction-log entry.

  2. Two sessions cannot conflict in git over a file that does not exist yet, so both get written and you find out when a human reads the folder. A shared file saying who is doing what, committed and pushed, is enough. It is a message, not a lock — and the point is that the second person finds out before doing the work rather than after.

    There is one file in the repository that answers “is anyone else in here?”, and opening a claim in it takes under a minute.

Phase 7 · A name of your own

30 min, then a wait

Only when you have something to put on it. Three companies, each doing one job: Porkbun sells you the name, Cloudflare answers questions about where it points, and GitHub serves the pages. Keeping those three jobs separate in your head is most of the difficulty.

  1. porkbun.com. Two things to look at that the checkout does not put in front of you: the renewal price, not the first-year price — on some endings those differ by a factor of ten — and whether WHOIS privacy is included, which at Porkbun it is. Decline the rest of the upsells; you can add any of them later and will probably never want to.

    A name you will still want in five years beats a clever one. It goes on papers.

    The domain is in your account and you know what it costs next year, not just this year.

  2. DNS is the phone book: it turns your name into the address of a machine. Porkbun can run it, and Cloudflare is worth the extra step because everything later — a second site, a subdomain, mail, a redirect — is done in one place, and it is free.

    Add the site at Cloudflare; it reads your existing records and then gives you two nameservers. Paste those into Porkbun’s authoritative-nameserver field, replacing what is there. That single change is the whole handover. (Cloudflare’s own instructions.)

    Propagation is usually minutes and occasionally hours. Nothing is broken while you wait; it is a queue, not an error.

    Cloudflare’s dashboard says the zone is Active. Until it says that, nothing below will work and re-doing it will not help.

  3. Serving the site from Cloudflare itself — Pages or Workers — skips the next two steps entirely. The domain is already in the account doing the serving, so the custom domain is one button and the certificate is somebody else’s problem. It is the shorter road, and worth knowing before you walk the long one.

    One thing it costs: the deploy tool signs in through a browser, once per machine, and that step cannot be scripted or handed to an agent. Budget a minute for it on every new computer.

    You have decided who serves the site before wiring anything, rather than discovering the decision halfway through the certificate step.

  4. Two ends to connect, and both are needed. At GitHub: repository → Settings → Pages → Custom domain. That writes a file called CNAME into the repository, which is how the server knows this domain is yours.

    At Cloudflare, add the records:

    www   CNAME  USERNAME.github.io

the bare name (example.com) needs either
  ALIAS / ANAME  → USERNAME.github.io
or four A records:
  185.199.108.153   185.199.110.153
  185.199.109.153   185.199.111.153

    Cloudflare supports the ALIAS form, so prefer it — four literal addresses are four things that can change under you. (GitHub’s record reference; Porkbun’s version, if you skip Cloudflare and keep DNS at Porkbun.)

    GitHub’s Pages settings shows the domain with a green tick beside it, and the name loads in a browser.

  5. This is where this chain fails, and it fails silently. Cloudflare proxies by default — the orange cloud beside each record. With it on, GitHub’s request to prove you own the domain never reaches GitHub, so the certificate is never issued, and Enforce HTTPS in the Pages settings simply stays greyed out with no error anywhere explaining why.

    Set the records to DNS only — the grey cloud — and wait. Fifteen minutes to an hour is normal, and GitHub says up to 24. If it will not budge, remove the custom domain in the Pages settings and add it again; that re-triggers the request.

    Once Enforce HTTPS is tickable, tick it. Only then turn the orange cloud back on if you want Cloudflare’s caching in front of the site — and it is genuinely optional.

    https:// works, and the Enforce HTTPS box is ticked rather than greyed out.

Three ways this list goes wrong

Each of these is a fix that feels like a fix. Each one below it is something that actually happened, and each links to the full account.

  • Setting up the guards firstWrong

    Phase 5 looks like the enjoyable part and it is the one part you cannot do in advance. Every guard built before its failure is a guess, and guesses accumulate into a setup that costs more to maintain than the work it was protecting.

    What actually happened. Across one estate, the scripts that run automatically when a session opens had grown to 39, 34, 27, 17, 11, 9 and 7 kilobytes. One kept growing until sessions stopped opening at all, against a sixty-second ceiling the editor hardcodes. Raising the script’s own timeout changed nothing — it was never the thing enforcing the limit. The guard had eaten the thing it was guarding.

    Instead: build a guard only from friction that has actually happened to you, and keep its off switch somewhere the broken thing cannot reach — a switch inside the project is unreachable when the broken thing is what opens the project.

    The full incident — It Looked Right, challenge 4

  • “Remember this” as a fixWrong

    You find the mistake, you discover the better way, and you tell the agent to remember it. A request is not a rule. A project instructions file is not enforceable; use all the emphasis you like and the second sentence still gets skipped.

    What actually happened. A hold was written into a project’s instructions: queue page changes, do not publish them one at a time. It was written down clearly. It lost — because three separate machines in that project tell a session to publish, and none of them waits to be asked: a ready-to-paste command in the staleness report, a daily workflow summary, and a line in every session briefing. The session that publishes is right by every signal available to it. Nobody was careless. The repair was to move the hold to where those three signals are computed, so all three now print the hold instead of the publish command.

    Instead: ask what tier your fix is on. Prose in an instructions file costs nothing to ignore. A checklist costs nothing when you are busy. A test costs a red line. Structure means the wrong thing is not reachable. Move the fix down that list until it can no longer be skipped — usually to wherever the wrong behaviour is being suggested from.

    The one exception, and it is worth knowing: a definition holds where an instruction does not. An instruction competes with what the model already believes and loses. A glossary the session reads before it reasons is the belief it starts from, so there is nothing to lose to.

    The full incident, and the four tiers — It Looked Right, Fixes that hold

  • Reading the reply instead of the resultWrong

    The explanation will sound right. It is written to sound right, and it is your only evidence of how you were understood — which is not evidence that anything happened. A green check is evidence about the check, not about the work.

    What actually happened. A complaint came in about a job. Jobs have numbers. The person answering meant to type the below job number, and typed the blow job number instead. Spell check passed it: “blow” is a word. Grammar check passed it: the sentence parses. Both tools did exactly what they are built to do, and neither was built to ask whether this is the word you meant. It was caught by the recipient.

    Instead: open the artifact yourself, and when you rely on an automatic check, break it on purpose once and confirm it notices. A check that has never failed has not been shown to work — it has been shown to pass.

    The full incident, and the errors worth naming — It Looked Right, challenge 3
