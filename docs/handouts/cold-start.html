Cold Start

An email address to a first agent session that leaves a record behind it — by browser, by laptop, or by cluster. Every step in order, and the condition that tells you each one worked.

None of this is coding, and none of it is optional polish. Every item is a decision the agent will otherwise make for you, silently, in your first hour — where your data lives, what it may run without asking, whether tonight’s work reaches tomorrow’s machine.

Every step ends with a “done when”. An action and its report are two different events. Something that says it worked is telling you it reached the end of its own instructions, not that anything happened. Checking the world instead of the summary is the whole skill; these steps are just where you start practising it.

One question, asked at the door: how far are you going?

This decides which steps you are shown. Pick the smallest one that gets you what you want — you can move up at any time, the page keeps every box you have ticked, and moving up only ever adds steps.

The two questions underneath this one
One machine, or more than one?
More than one and a remote stops being optional. Work that lives only in a folder on one laptop reaches no other machine and no other session — and you find that out on the day you need it.
Alone, or with other people?
Working with anyone — including your own second session running in another window — adds Phase 6. Alone on one laptop, you can skip Phase 6 entirely and most of step 1.3.

Answer “more than one” or “with other people” to either, and you are on the third tier whatever else you had in mind. That is the whole of what the choice above is asking.

    Two things to settle before you start, because both are annoying to undo. The username is the one you will be known by, and it ends up inside every link anybody saves to your work — changing it later breaks those links for other people, not for you, so you never find out. Pick one you would be content to have on a paper.     And ask first if your work touches research data or human subjects. Your university or employer may run its own host — usually a private GitLab — and may require you to use it. That is a governance question, not a preference, and it is much cheaper to ask now than to move a repository later.

But it is what gives your project a second place to exist. Without it, everything you and the agent make lives in one folder on one laptop: it reaches no other machine, no collaborator, and no future session — and none of that announces itself. The short way · a web app, in the browser

one evening

Five steps, one evening, all of it in a browser. Nothing to install, no terminal, and no git init. You finish with a working page at an address you can send to somebody. It was walked start to finish on 2026-08-30, and the places it went wrong are written into the steps rather than tidied out of them.

The fifth step is not the end of anything. It is where the loop closes. From then on you ask for the next item on your list, look at it, and it is live — and that round trip is now minutes rather than a project. The reason to get published early is that everything after it gets cheaper, not that you are done. Go back to the list you wrote in W2 and take the next one.

None of what follows is a prerequisite for that. You can keep building here indefinitely. The rest of this page is for when you want the machine underneath it — your own editor, your own copy of the files, work that outlives a browser tab, other people — and you can pick VS Code, on my laptop at the top on any day you like. Everything you have ticked here stays ticked.

  2. Incomplete

    A repository is just a folder that lives on github.com instead of on your laptop, and keeps a record of every change made to it. You already have the account from step 1.2; this makes the folder. You will not type a single command.

    On github.com: New repository → tick “Add a README” → Create. That was the path on 2026-08-30. The tick is the part that matters: it puts one file inside, and a completely empty repository is not something Claude can open. Keep it private — you can make it public later in one click, and the reverse is not true of anything anybody has already copied.

    Then go to claude.ai/code, connect your GitHub account, and pick the repository you just made. Paste the sentence you wrote in W2 and ask for the first item on your list.

A folder on your laptop means dragging it somewhere by hand, every single time, forever. That is the whole reason step 1.2 was not optional.

  4. Incomplete

    Ask for the first item on your list. Only that one. Not the whole sentence from W2, and not two items because they seem related — one. You are about to check the result by hand, and that is only possible when you know what you asked for.

    When it says it has finished, do not take its word for it. Type this:

    “List every file you changed, and say in one line why each one needed to change.”

    You are not checking whether the code is right. You are checking whether it touched anything you did not ask for — a setting, a second file, something it decided to tidy. That question needs no programming at all, and it is the one that catches the expensive mistakes. If something on the list surprises you, ask why it is there before going on.

    Then go and look at the thing yourself. Open the page. Use the new thing. Watch the recording appear. A message saying it works is a report that it reached the end of its own instructions — which is a different event from the thing working, and the two come apart more often than you would expect.

    If it tells you to open something like http://localhost:8000, that is your own computer talking to itself. The page is real and it works — but it exists only on your machine, only while that thing is running, and only for you. Send that address to a colleague and they get an error; close the window and it is gone. This is normal and correct at this stage, and it is not publishing. Local means your machine. Putting it where other people can open it is a separate act, and it is W5. The word to watch for is localhost, or an address starting 127.0.0.1: both mean here, not the internet.

    When you are happy, tell it to save: “commit and push this.” The change is now on github.com, with a note of what it was, and you can get back to this version later even if the next thing goes wrong.

    • I asked it to list every file it changed, and nothing on the list surprised me
    • I opened it myself and watched the new thing work — I did not just read a message saying it did
    • If somebody asked me what changed just now, I could tell them in one sentence

Three ways this list goes wrong

    
Each of these is a fix that feels like a fix. Each one below it is something that actually happened, and each links to the full account.

    
    
      
  • 
        Setting up the guards firstWrong
        
    Phase 5 looks like the enjoyable part and it is the one part you cannot do in advance. Every guard built before its failure is a guess, and guesses accumulate into a setup that costs more to maintain than the work it was protecting.
    
        
    What actually happened. Across one estate, the scripts that run automatically when a session opens had grown to 39, 34, 27, 17, 11, 9 and 7 kilobytes. One kept growing until sessions stopped opening at all, against a sixty-second ceiling the editor hardcodes. Raising the script’s own timeout changed nothing — it was never the thing enforcing the limit. The guard had eaten the thing it was guarding.
    
        
    Instead: build a guard only from friction that has actually happened to you, and keep its off switch somewhere the broken thing cannot reach — a switch inside the project is unreachable when the broken thing is what opens the project.
    
        
    The full incident — It Looked Right, challenge 4
    
      
    • 
      
  • 
        “Remember this” as a fixWrong
        
    You find the mistake, you discover the better way, and you tell the agent to remember it. A request is not a rule. A project instructions file is not enforceable; use all the emphasis you like and the second sentence still gets skipped.
    
        
    What actually happened. A hold was written into a project’s instructions: queue page changes, do not publish them one at a time. It was written down clearly. It lost — because three separate machines in that project tell a session to publish, and none of them waits to be asked: a ready-to-paste command in the staleness report, a daily workflow summary, and a line in every session briefing. The session that publishes is right by every signal available to it. Nobody was careless. The repair was to move the hold to where those three signals are computed, so all three now print the hold instead of the publish command.
    
        
    Instead: ask what tier your fix is on. Prose in an instructions file costs nothing to ignore. A checklist costs nothing when you are busy. A test costs a red line. Structure means the wrong thing is not reachable. Move the fix down that list until it can no longer be skipped — usually to wherever the wrong behavior is being suggested from.
    
        
    The one exception, and it is worth knowing: a definition holds where an instruction does not. An instruction competes with what the model already believes and loses. A glossary the session reads before it reasons is the belief it starts from, so there is nothing to lose to.
    
        
    The full incident, and the four tiers — It Looked Right, Fixes that hold
    
      
    • 
      
  • 
        Reading the reply instead of the resultWrong
        
    The explanation will sound right. It is written to sound right, and it is your only evidence of how you were understood — which is not evidence that anything happened. A green check is evidence about the check, not about the work.
    
        
    What actually happened. A complaint came in about a job. Jobs have numbers. The person answering meant to type the below job number, and typed the blow job number instead. Spell check passed it: “blow” is a word. Grammar check passed it: the sentence parses. Both tools did exactly what they are built to do, and neither was built to ask whether this is the word you meant. It was caught by the recipient.
    
        
    Instead: open the artifact yourself, and when you rely on an automatic check, break it on purpose once and confirm it notices. A check you have not seen fail has not been shown to work — it has been shown to pass.
