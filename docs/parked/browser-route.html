Five steps, one evening, all of it in a browser. Nothing to install, nothing to configure, and not one command to type. You finish with a working page at an address you can send to somebody. The browser route was walked start to finish on 2026-08-30, and the places the walk went wrong are written into the steps rather than tidied out of them. What it needs from you is one thing: an agent that makes what you ask for, rather than helping you make it yourself. The test that matters is who is doing the typing. Every step from W1 to W5 was walked on claude.ai and names that one company’s screens throughout; any chat agent that can hold a repository and write files does the same job under another name. What will not is an agent built into a document, a mailbox or a company’s app suite — the sort that lives in Word, Teams, Outlook or an intranet portal. Ask one of those to build something and it does what it is for: it opens a blank template and has you type the title and place the boxes. Every screen is correct, nothing is broken, nobody has made a mistake — and you are the one making the thing, which is the opposite of what the rest of this page is about. There is nothing to check when you already know what it says.

So, before anything else: “Make a file called hello.txt with the word hello in it, and show me the file.” A file comes back → tick it in W1 and everything opens. Instructions for making one, a template to fill in, or a new app to click through → wrong kind of agent, and these five steps would keep almost working, which is worse than failing outright. Try one of the agents linked at step 1.1, where you get an agent, instead; and if an agent built into an office suite is the only agent you are permitted, this route is not open to you today — take the laptop route at the top of the page, where the agent works on your own files. Taking the laptop route is a real answer and it costs a minute instead of an evening.

This is not hypothetical. On 2 September 2026 a beginner walked these steps with the agent her institution had given her. That agent put her in a form builder, typing a title onto a blank template. About fifteen screenshots, most of an hour lost connecting a cloud folder, no repository at the end of it, and the record of the session copied by hand into a note. Nothing on this page told her she was in the wrong kind of product, and every answer she got was helpful and correct.

W5, the fifth step, where you put your page on the internet, is not the end of anything. It is where the loop closes. From then on you ask for the next item on your list, look at it, and it is live — and that round trip is now minutes rather than a project. The reason to get published early is that everything after it gets cheaper, not that you are done. Go back to the list you wrote in W2 and take the next one.

Nothing on this page after W5 is a prerequisite for going on building in the browser. You can keep building here indefinitely. The rest of this page is for when you want the machine underneath what you have built — your own editor, your own copy of the files, work that outlives a browser tab, other people — and you can switch to the laptop route at the top of the page on any day you like. Everything you have ticked here stays ticked.

  3. Incomplete

    Not open yet. W1’s file test has not been ticked, and this step is where you hand your agent your whole project — worth sixty seconds of proof first. Ask your agent for hello.txt, tick the box in W1, and this opens.

    A repository is just a folder that lives on github.com instead of on your laptop, and keeps a record of every change made to the folder. You already have the GitHub account from step 1.2; this step makes the folder. You will not type a single command.

    On github.com: New repository → tick “Add a README” → Create. (A README is just a plain text file describing what the thing is; every repository is expected to have one, and GitHub shows it on the front page.) That was the click path on 2026-08-30. The tick is the part that matters: it puts one file inside, and a completely empty repository is not something an agent can open. Keep the repository private — you can make it public later in one click, and the reverse is not true of anything anybody has already copied.

    Then go to claude.ai/code, connect your GitHub account, and pick the repository you just made. Paste the sentence you wrote in W2 and ask for the first item on the list of parts you wrote underneath it.

    Why a repository rather than just showing your agent a folder on your machine. The Claude desktop app can do that, and it works. But at W5, where you put your page on the internet, the repository is what makes publishing keep happening on its own — every later change goes up without you remembering to do anything. A folder on your laptop means dragging it somewhere by hand, every single time, forever. That is the whole reason step 1.2, the GitHub account, was not optional.

  4. Incomplete

    Not open yet, and this is the step worth waiting for. This is where the evening goes, so it needs both cheap tests passed first: W1 — your agent made you a file rather than handing you a template — and W3 — something it wrote actually landed on github.com and you saw it there. Until the W3 test holds, work done here lives in a chat window and saving it means copying it out by hand.

    Ask for the first item on the list of parts you wrote in W2. Only that one. Not the whole sentence from W2, and not two items because they seem related — one. You are about to check the result by hand, and that is only possible when you know what you asked for.

    When the agent says it has finished, do not take its word for it. Type this:

    “List every file you changed, and say in one line why each one needed to change.”

    You are not checking whether the code is right. You are checking whether the agent touched anything you did not ask for — a setting, a second file, something it decided to tidy. That question needs no programming at all, and it is the one that catches the expensive mistakes. If a file on the agent’s list surprises you, ask why it is there before going on.

    Then go and look at the thing yourself. Open the page. Use the new part. If your first item was show one recording, watch a recording appear. A message saying it works is a report that the agent reached the end of its own instructions — which is a different event from the thing working, and the two come apart more often than you would expect.

    If the agent tells you to open something like http://localhost:8000, that is one machine talking to itself — yours, or the one your agent is working on. The page is real and it works, but it exists only on that machine, only while the program the agent started is running, and only for whoever is sitting at that machine. Send that address to a colleague and they get an error; close the window and the page is gone. This is normal and correct at this stage, and it is not publishing. Local means this machine, not the internet. Putting the page where other people can open it is a separate act, and it is W5, the publishing step. The word to watch for is localhost, or an address starting 127.0.0.1: both mean here, not the internet.

    When you are happy, tell the agent to save: “commit and push this.” Those are the two words for saving, and they are worth knowing because the rest of this page uses them. A commit is one saved version with a short note attached saying what changed — the note is the point of a commit. Push sends the commit up to github.com, so the work exists somewhere other than where you are sitting. You can get back to this exact version later even if the next thing goes wrong. (Just Enough Git defines these and the ten other words this page uses, in one page, and says what it leaves out.)

    You will also see GitHub Pages offered — github.com’s own way of publishing a repository as a page — and it is free too.
