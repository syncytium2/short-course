Workshop proposal · winter term

A Day With Coding Agents

One day, one room, and everybody leaves with a working tool that solves a problem they actually have — built by an agent, checked by the person who knows the subject. Not a demo. Not a lecture about AI.

Who this is for

And who it is not for.

Researchers who have already tried this and concluded it does not work. That group is larger than the enthusiast group and it is the one worth the day, because their objection is usually correct about what happened and wrong about what it meant.

Two recorded examples, both from this department’s own kind of work. Someone wanted the tick labels on a fold-change axis recomputed on a log scale, put it to a search engine, got nothing usable, and concluded “AI is stupid, it can’t do this simple task.” Someone else tried to get a chat window to draw a genogram; it described one fluently and drew nothing.

Neither person met an agent. They met a search box and a chat window, which are different products with different jobs. The day exists to make that difference something people have seen happen rather than been told.

Before the day

About forty minutes, done at home. If this is not finished, the morning is lost to installation.

Each item is written as something that must be true, with the check that proves it. A participant who cannot complete one arrives and says so, and we fix it in the first fifteen minutes rather than at minute five of the first exercise.

  • You have an agent, and you have signed in with an account you mean to keep. True when: you asked it for a file called hello.txt and a file came back. Instructions for making one, or a template to fill in yourself, means you have the wrong kind of product — tell us before the day, not on it.
  • The agent can see files on your own machine. True when: you pointed it at a folder and it told you something about the contents that you had not typed into it.
  • You have brought a real problem, in writing, one paragraph. True when: a colleague could read it and say what the finished thing would do. Best case: something you already failed to get out of a chat window.
  • You have brought whatever the problem needs — a spreadsheet, a figure, a set of numbers. True when: the file opens on your laptop, in the room, offline.

What the department is asked for

One billing code, one eligibility answer, and a room with power.

The ask

A Shortcode to bill the day’s usage against, and a decision about who in the room is eligible to use it. Nothing else is required from the department: no licences to buy, no software to procure, no IT ticket.

The University already provides a free chat assistant to everyone, and puts the agent behind an employment check and a billing code. That is the distinction this workshop teaches, enforced by the institution itself — which is better evidence than any demonstration I could run.

The day

Two builds, one break, and a demonstration that is allowed to fail in front of the room.

9:00 The same question, three places A search engine, a chat window, and an agent get one identical prompt — a literature question with a citation that either resolves or does not. Run live, unrehearsed. If the chat window happens not to fabricate that morning, we say so out loud; a day about checking claims cannot rig its own opening.
9:45 Why serious people bounce off this The four barriers, and the one that actually stops people: not knowing whether the thing it handed you is right. Short, and the only lecture of the day.
10:15 Build, first pass — commission the thing Everyone describes their own problem to their own agent and asks for a single self-contained page that solves it. One file, opens in a browser, no server and nothing installed. By the break it runs and is wrong somewhere.
12:00 Lunch
13:00 Build, second pass — find what is wrong with it Paired: one drives, one is the designated sceptic, swap halfway. You are checking against what you know about the subject, not reading the code. The worked example in front of the room is a physiological feedback loop — set point, sensor, error, effector, gain — because a baroreflex does not hunt with a forty-second period, and everybody here knows that whether or not they can read a line of JavaScript. That is the one arrangement in the day where the person is the expert and the machine is the novice.
14:30 How this goes wrong in the wild Real incidents with receipts: the check that could not fire, the number that was right about the wrong thing, the report that said done when nothing had happened. Every one from working projects, with the commit that fixed it.
15:15 Monday morning What you write down so the next session — yours or an agent’s — can pick the work up. Three lines at the end of every sitting. Then everyone opens the thing they built.
16:00 Close

What people leave with

  • A working tool of their own — one file, on their laptop, that solves a problem they brought in that morning. It runs without the internet and without an account.
  • A record of how it was made, including where the agent got it wrong and what the person did about it.
  • One habit: check the thing itself, not the report about the thing. This is the only part of the day that does not expire when the software changes.

Not settled

Listed here rather than discovered later.

  • Who in the room is eligible. Agent access at this institution is faculty and staff only, students excluded, sharing prohibited. If graduate students are in the audience, they need a separate route and it has to be arranged in advance. This is the question that decides whether the day is possible as written.
  • The cost is an estimate and not a measurement. One run settles it, for a few dollars.
  • How many people, which sets the budget. Every cost figure above is per person; the room total follows from a headcount nobody has set yet. It also sets the shape of the afternoon — pairing works in a room of twenty and stops working in a room of sixty.
  • Room requirements. Power at every seat, and a network that permits the agent to reach the internet — worth confirming rather than assuming.
