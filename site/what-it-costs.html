What It Costs

What a working setup actually costs, who ends up paying, and which part of it is genuinely free.

Version 0.1.12 · 31 Aug 2026First published 29 Aug 2026

Four things in Cold Start can cost money, and it is worth having them in front of you before you start rather than meeting them one at a time:

  • The agent — free tiers exist and are real, but they are the part you outgrow first This is the whole of the rest of this page, because it is the only one where the amount is open-ended.
  • Synced storage — free up to a point, and the point is lower than people expect Dropbox’s free tier is 2 GB; Google and Microsoft give more. The review folder in Cold Start 2.2 usually fits. Anything holding real data does not, which is the same reason 2.3 tells you to keep the data root out of the sync client — a rule that is about reliability and happens to also be the thing keeping this line cheap. Paid personal tiers start around $10 a month for terabytes.
  • A domain name, if you want a web address of your own — Cold Start Phase 7 Registrars charge per year: roughly $10–15 for a common ending, and wildly more for a fashionable one. The renewal price is the number that matters, not the first year. Hosting the site is free; the name is not.
  • Research storage or a cluster, and most people need neither — Cold Start 1.3 Usually sponsored by a lab or department rather than bought by a person, which makes it an access question more than a price one.

Everything else is free and stays free: the record, the history, the editor, and somewhere to put a web page. You can get to a working setup without paying anyone — everything through Phase 6, on a free agent tier. Two caveats the runbook makes plain: its worked commands assume Claude Code, which has no free tier, so on a free agent you substitute the equivalents; and Phase 7 needs a domain, which is the one part that costs money. What you cannot do for free is use it heavily. So the decision that matters is not which agent to buy. It is which shape of bill you agree to — and there are three, only one of which can stop.

Every figure on this page was read on 29 August 2026 and will be wrong before you need it. Plans get renamed, split, re-metered and re-priced faster than anything else here — one of them was split in two four months before this was written.

Treat the numbers as orders of magnitude and the links as the authority. When this matters, go and look at the vendor’s page. Nothing on this page re-checks itself, and when it rots it will not tell you.

What this assumes you already have

A computer, an internet connection you are not paying for by the megabyte, and an email address. These are treated as given from here on.

Worth saying once, because it is the first place the ground is uneven: the free floor below is free only on top of several hundred dollars of hardware and a monthly bill. That is not a small assumption, and everything after this makes it.

The free part, and it stays free

  • git, the thing that keeps the history Free and open source. It is a program on your computer, not a service.
  • A GitHub account Free, including unlimited repositories — public or private. Some collaboration features on private repositories sit on the paid tiers, so check the plan page before you build a group around one. Nothing in Cold Start needs a paid tier.
  • An editor VS Code and every serious alternative are free.
  • Hosting a website Cloudflare’s free tier serves a site for nothing, from a private repository or from no repository at all — which is why Cold Start 7.3 sends you there. GitHub Pages is also free, but only from a public repository; a private one needs a paid GitHub plan. The name is the part that costs — see above. Without one your site still works; it just lives at an address ending in github.io.

That is most of the setup, and it is genuinely, permanently free rather than free-for-now. None of it has a trial period and none of it is a tier you get moved off.

The one thing that costs: the agent

Every one of these is sold in one of three shapes, and the shape matters more than the number. Learn to ask which one you are being handed, because you will not be told unprompted.

Shape 1 — a ceiling

A subscription

You pay a fixed amount each month. When you have used your allowance, it stops and tells you when it resets. Some plans let you deliberately buy past the limit; that is a decision you make, not a default.

Worst case: you cannot work for a few hours.

Shape 2 — a meter

Pay per use, on your own card

You are billed for what you consume. There is no natural stopping point — nothing pauses, nothing warns, and a loop left running overnight is billed the same as work. Most such accounts can be given a spend limit. Few people set one.

Worst case: a number you did not agree to, arriving after the fact.

Shape 3 — somebody else’s meter

An institutional billing code

The same meter, pointed at a department. It feels free, because you do not enter a card. It is not free, it is usually uncapped, and the person who reads the statement is not you — so the feedback arrives weeks later and lands on somebody who did not do the spending.

Worst case: a conversation with your head of department about a number.

If you are paying for this yourself, buy a ceiling. Not because it is cheaper — often it is not — but because the failure mode is “you stop for four hours” instead of “you owe money you did not decide to spend”. That difference is worth more than the price gap, especially in the first month when you have no instinct for what a task costs.

And a meter is the correct choice for some people. Just choose it, rather than being handed it.

What heavy use actually looks like

Anthropic’s own cost documentation reports that across enterprise deployments the average is around $13 per developer per active day, and $150–250 per developer per month, with 90% of users staying under $30 on an active day.

That is not you, and reading it as if it were is the mistake this section exists to prevent. Those are professional software engineers using an agent as their primary tool, all day, every working day. A researcher who uses one for a few hours a week is nowhere near it.

The figure is useful for one thing only: it is roughly the ceiling of what a single very heavy human being can spend. If your bill approaches it, you are not being unlucky, you are working like a full-time engineer.

A worked example: one adversarial review

Abstract monthly figures are hard to act on. Here is one real, repeated task, measured rather than estimated — a document put through an eleven-agent adversarial review, where each agent reads independently so that none of them can be talked round by the others.

You can go and look at the task before you judge the price. The review is syncytium2/murderboard, Apache‑2.0 — the eleven roles, what each one is told to do, and the prompt that runs them are all in that repository. It matters that this is checkable, because “$26 for a review” means nothing until you know how much reviewing that buys. Eleven independent readers going through a document line by line is a different purchase from one model skimming it, and the price is only defensible next to the work.

That is the number worth carrying around, because it is the shape of every real task: not a monthly subscription, but a few dollars for a serious piece of work, and a few cents for a small one. A month is just however many of those you did.

Two things this example teaches that the price alone does not.

The expensive part is the part that works. Eleven agents cost roughly eleven times one agent, and the reason to pay it is that they cannot see each other’s reasoning. One agent walking the same eleven checklists costs an eleventh as much and shares a mind with the author, so it agrees with itself. Independence is the product, and independence is the line item.

It can also run away. The process allows the review to repeat until it converges, so the ceiling for one document is four rounds — call it $64–160 at the larger model’s rates. One recorded run went fourteen rounds before a person stopped it. Nothing was broken. Nobody was watching.

And the measurement itself is a caution. Before this was measured, the same review was reported at 833,142 tokens by the agent that ran it, described as measured. It was not measured — it was asserted, confidently, and it was 1.9× too low. The real figure only appeared when somebody totted up the token log afterwards.

If you take one thing from this page: a number an agent gives you about its own consumption is a claim, not a receipt. The receipt is on the usage screen.

What one teaching session costs

The one case anybody here has costed: six students, one ninety-minute session. It is worth doing twice, because the two ways of sizing it disagree — and the disagreement is the whole argument of this page in miniature.

Priced as a plan. The university sizes its classroom allocation per user per month, for a term. One session is a fraction of a month, which puts it at an order of $5–15 a student — $30–90 for the room.

Priced as a meter. Ninety minutes is not ninety minutes of billing. It is a handful of short requests each, separated by talking, watching and typing. At the per-task rates established above — a few cents for a small task, single dollars for a serious one — six students doing beginner-sized things for an hour and a half plausibly consume a few dollars in total for the whole room, not per student.

The gap between those two answers is not an error. One is what the institution sets aside; the other is what actually gets used. A plan is a ceiling you have bought whether or not you reach it, and a meter is a bill for what you did. Budget with the first number and expect the second — and if you are choosing between them for a class, the ceiling is the one that cannot surprise you in front of six students.

Either way the conclusion is the same and it is the point of this section: at this size the cost question answers itself, and the decision becomes a pedagogical one — which is the opposite of the conclusion this material reached before anyone actually looked.

Both numbers are estimates, and the first one rests on a figure this project has not confirmed. The per-user monthly allocation is reported as $50 in secondary coverage; the university’s own pricing page states no amount. Nobody here has provisioned a classroom and read an invoice. The second number is an extrapolation from tasks of a different shape — the measured runs above are one long adversarial review, not six beginners taking turns.

They are recorded anyway, because the estimate already changed a decision, and an estimate that changes a decision belongs somewhere the decision can be revisited. One measured session is still owed — and the way to settle it is the usage screen after the first real class, not a better guess.

What actually drives the number

The useful thing about the bill is that you have more control over it than the pricing page suggests. Four things move it, in roughly this order:

  • How long the conversation has been open Your entire conversation is re-sent on every single request. A one-line question in a session you have had open all day carries the whole day with it. Starting a fresh session between unrelated tasks costs nothing and is the largest lever you have.
  • Which model The largest models cost several times the smaller ones for the same request. Most work does not need the largest.
  • How many agents at once Each one runs its own conversation, so the cost scales with the number of them and how long each lives. This is the fastest way to be surprised.
  • Vagueness “Improve this code” makes it read everything. A specific request reads two files. The cheap prompt and the good prompt are the same prompt.

The equity problem, stated plainly

This is the part that usually goes unsaid, and it should not, because it changes what you are allowed to assign to other people.

The free tier is not the same tool

“There is a free tier” is true and it is not the whole truth. Free tiers are rate-limited and often run smaller models. The difference that matters is not quality per answer — it is how many attempts you get before you have to stop. Since the entire method is spec, check, re-spec, a person with fewer attempts is not doing a slightly worse version of the work. They are doing a different, shorter thing, and they will conclude they are bad at it.

The institutional route is not evenly available

Checked at one large public university in August 2026: every student gets a chat box for free, and the agent sits behind an employment check and a departmental billing code — staff and faculty only, students excluded, sharing prohibited. A graduate student employed as a research or teaching assistant can often be sponsored; a student on a taught course generally cannot.

So the institution itself draws a line between chat and agent, and it draws it along employment status. That is a better demonstration of the difference between the two than any classroom demo — and it is also a line straight through the middle of a cohort.

Student credit programs have borders

The vendor credit schemes that look like the obvious answer carry eligibility conditions that are easy to miss. One offering $100 of credits to students, verified against a university email address, is United States and Canada only. In an international research group that excludes a real fraction of the room, and it excludes them quietly, one at a time, at the point of sign-up.

If you are teaching, you are assigning an expense

Set work that requires an agent and you have set work that costs money, and it will not fall evenly. The people it falls hardest on are the least likely to say so. Three things follow:

  • Find out what it costs before you assign it, not after Measure one real session. An estimate you have not tested is how a course accidentally becomes means-tested.
  • Provision centrally if you possibly can One billing arrangement you control beats twenty personal cards, and it is the only version where you can actually see what was spent.
  • Do not let the free tier be the punishment Pairing works, and it is good pedagogy in its own right — two people arguing about what the agent just did is the exercise. But choose it as pedagogy. If it is quietly the contingency for whoever could not pay, everyone in the room can tell.

And the uncomfortable one

Where spending is uncapped, the amount spent tracks who feels entitled to spend it rather than who needs it. Some organizations have gone as far as ranking people by token consumption — which measures budget and confidence, not work, and rewards exactly the person who least needed encouragement. If a number is going to be visible, make it the work.

What to do

  1. Start on the free floor and stay there until it hurts twice. Not once. Twice, on real work, doing something you needed to do anyway. Hitting a limit while playing is not evidence of anything.
  2. If it is your own money, buy a ceiling. The cheapest paid tier first. You can move up later; the instinct for what a task costs takes about a month to develop and it is cheaper to develop it behind a limit.
  3. If it is a meter, set the spend limit the same day you set up the account. Before the first real session, while you still remember it exists. This is a five-minute job that stops the only genuinely bad outcome on this page.
  4. If it is institutional, ask who reads the statement. In writing, before the first long session: is there a cap, who sees the number, and what happens if it is larger than expected. “Nobody mentioned a limit” is not the same as “there is no limit”.
  5. Look at your own usage in the first week. Not the third month. Cold Start 4.6.
